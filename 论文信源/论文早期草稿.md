\section{Introduction and Motivation}

  Recent progress in large language model (LLM) agents has been driven by their ability to interact with external tools and execute multi-step plans in realistic environments. In software engineering, SWE-bench formalizes the task of resolving real GitHub issues by requiring agents to produce code changes that pass repository tests, thus grounding evaluation in executable correctness rather than purely textual plausibility. 
  Meanwhile, OSWorld extends evaluation to open-ended computer-use tasks within real desktop environments, emphasizing multimodal perception, long-horizon decision-making, and robust interaction with diverse applications. 

  Despite these advances, practical failures of long-horizon agents frequently arise not from the inability to generate correct local actions, but from breakdowns in procedural reliability: agents repeat invalid actions, neglect essential checks, mis-handle environmental assumptions, and propagate early mistakes into cascading failure chains. This observation is reinforced by research demonstrating that workflow and interface engineering can produce substantial gains. For instance, SWE-agent shows that the design of an Agent-Computer Interface (ACI) significantly improves performance by structuring how agents edit files, navigate repositories, and execute commands.  At the same time, Agentless highlights that strong performance can arise from a simplified, interpretable pipeline (localization followed by repair) rather than increasingly complex autonomy, suggesting that reliable procedures and verification often dominate marginal gains from additional agent complexity. 

  A further challenge concerns the reliability of benchmarks themselves. OpenAI’s SWE-bench Verified documents that evaluation artifacts—such as underspecified issues or insufficient tests—can systematically distort conclusions about agent competence, motivating a stronger emphasis on robust harnesses and process-level diagnostics, rather than success rate alone.  These trends collectively motivate a research direction centered on *procedural reliability* as a first-class objective: instead of treating failures as isolated errors to be patched by ad hoc prompting, we seek a principled mechanism that allows agents to accumulate, validate, and operationalize experience over time.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Gap}

  Existing “training-free” agent improvements largely fall into three categories. First, workflow/interface scaffolding structures how agents interact with tools and environments, often yielding large gains but leaving the agent vulnerable to repeated procedural mistakes when conditions shift.  Second, reflection and textual memory frameworks store linguistic summaries of failures and improvements, enabling better subsequent decisions without updating model weights. Third, retrieval-augmented methods—including graph-augmented retrieval—aim to improve access to relevant information and relations, but often do not enforce correctness or prevent risky actions beyond prompting. 

  Across these lines, “experience” is commonly represented as unstructured text or transient traces, which makes it difficult to ensure that reused experience is (i) grounded in replayable evidence, (ii) robust under distribution shift, and (iii) enforceable as a verifiable control signal rather than merely a suggestion. In long-horizon settings, this gap manifests as repeated invalid actions, delayed error detection, and cascading failures that could have been prevented by earlier procedural constraints. The absence of a systematic pathway from empirical experience to verifiable decision-time guardrails is therefore a key research opportunity.

\section{Proposed Research: Agent-mem}

  This proposal introduces **Agent-mem**, a training-free “Work-Experience Memory” paradigm designed to turn agent trajectories into **auditable, evolvable, and progressively verifiable** experience assets. The central thesis is that long-horizon reliability improves when experience is not merely retrieved as text, but represented and managed as an evidence-backed substrate that can progressively influence agent behavior through increasingly strong forms of control.

  Agent-mem is designed around two complementary uses of experience. First, during planning, it surfaces evidence-backed procedural guidance drawn from prior successful trajectories, aiming to reduce exploration noise and enforce critical checks early. Second, during execution, especially upon errors, it retrieves prior failure-and-recovery patterns to propose minimal repairs and validation steps, improving recovery success and limiting cascade depth. Over time, high-confidence experience can be promoted from soft guidance to soft control and, when justified, to hard fail-fast constraints. This progressive approach is motivated by the risks of over-constraining in diverse tasks and the need to maintain robustness under uncertainty.

  A key novelty of Agent-mem is the explicit focus on **verifiability**. Rather than treating experience solely as advice, Agent-mem investigates mechanisms for compiling mature experience into **preferences and constraints** that can be checked at decision time. We view PDDL3-style preference/soft-constraint semantics as an instance of this broader design space, enabling principled expressions of “should” versus “must-not” behavior while separating soft optimization from hard safety.  Importantly, the research framing is not tied to any single formalism; instead, it studies how progressively verifiable guardrails—whether implemented via PDDL-style constructs or lighter-weight rule systems—change outcomes and failure dynamics.

\section{ Research Questions and Hypotheses}

  The proposed work is organized around four research questions. The first asks whether auditable experience memory with progressive guardrails yields measurable improvements in success and reliability on execution-grounded benchmarks such as SWE-bench Verified and OSWorld.  The second asks how planning-time experience and execution-time recovery experience contribute independently and jointly, and whether they address different failure modes. The third asks whether progressively promoted constraints produce more stable improvements than purely textual memory, especially in terms of variance and robustness across tasks. The fourth asks whether Agent-mem improves efficiency—steps, tool calls, and cost—without sacrificing correctness, an increasingly important dimension in real-world computer-use settings. 

  These questions imply testable hypotheses: that Agent-mem reduces invalid actions and shifts failure onset later; that recovery-oriented memory primarily improves post-error recovery rates while planning-oriented memory reduces wasted exploration; and that progressive promotion to stronger guardrails increases stability compared to prompt-only baselines.

\section{Experimental Methodology and Evaluation Plan}

  Evaluation will prioritize benchmarks with reliable harnesses and executable correctness. SWE-bench will be used for baseline comparability, while SWE-bench Verified will serve as the primary SWE evaluation due to its stronger quality control and reduced artifact risk.  For GUI/computer-use, OSWorld provides realistic environments and diverse tasks suited for studying long-horizon procedural reliability and multimodal interaction.

  Crucially, the experimental design will go beyond end success rates. Agent-mem is explicitly motivated by process failures, so the evaluation will include process-level metrics such as invalid-action rate, constraint/preference violation rate (when enabled), the step at which the first major procedural violation occurs, and cascade depth measured as the propagation length from first critical error to final failure. These measures are intended to quantify whether Agent-mem changes the *failure dynamics* rather than merely shifting the final outcome by chance.

  Comparisons will include representative strong baselines that embody different philosophies: interface/workflow engineering (SWE-agent), simplified pipeline baselines (Agentless), and reflection-based textual memory (Reflexion). In addition, retrieval-centric approaches and graph-augmented retrieval provide a comparative lens on whether structuring retrieval alone suffices without verifiable control. 

  Ablations will isolate the impact of planning-time vs execution-time experience use, and the incremental impact of moving from soft guidance to soft control and to hard constraints. This incremental design is central to the research objective: it allows us to study not only whether guardrails help, but *when* they help, *how strongly* they should be applied, and how the system trades off conservatism and flexibility.

\section{Expected Contributions and Significance}

  This project aims to contribute a research framework for long-horizon LLM agents that operationalizes experience as an asset with three properties. First, **auditability** ensures experience reuse is evidence-backed rather than narrative-only. Second, **evolvability** enables experience to be refined and updated as conditions change. Third, **verifiability** establishes a path from empirical experience to decision-time constraints and preferences, enabling fail-fast behavior that prevents obvious invalid actions and limits cascade formation. The anticipated outcome is not merely higher pass rates, but a clearer scientific account of procedural reliability as a driver of long-horizon success.

  More broadly, by emphasizing SWE-bench Verified and OSWorld, the work aligns with community movement toward evaluation rigor and realistic interactive settings.  If successful, Agent-mem would offer a modular and benchmark-relevant route to improving reliability without model finetuning, complementing interface engineering and reflection methods while providing stronger guarantees than prompt-only experience replay.

\section{Risks and Mitigation Strategies}

  The principal risk is over-constraining behavior, which can prune valid solutions and reduce performance under distribution shift. This risk motivates the proposed progressive design in which experience is initially advisory and only later, under strong evidence, promoted to stronger control. Another risk is that experience signals may be noisy or non-stationary across repositories, applications, or toolchains, making naive reuse harmful. This will be mitigated by emphasizing evidence-backed experience, stratified evaluation across task slices, and sensitivity analysis of promotion thresholds. Finally, benchmark artifacts may distort conclusions, which motivates the decision to focus on SWE-bench Verified as a primary SWE benchmark and to report process-level metrics that remain informative even when end outcomes are noisy. 

\section{Conclusion}

  Agent-mem is proposed as a research program for improving long-horizon LLM agent reliability by transforming experience into auditable, evolvable, and progressively verifiable assets. By unifying planning-time guidance, execution-time recovery, and decision-time guardrails within a training-free paradigm, and by evaluating with robust harnesses and process-level metrics, the project aims to produce both empirical gains and a clearer scientific understanding of how procedural reliability governs success in interactive, executable benchmarks.
